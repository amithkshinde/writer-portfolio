Yugadi is a South Indian Hindu holiday celebrating the turnover of the Hindu lunisolar calendar, the annual shift in the moon’s orbit and the transition from winter to spring.

Yugadi refers to the era we are living in, i.e. the Kali Yuga, which is believed to have been started after Lord Krishna departed from the world. The event has been described by Maharshi Vedavyasa as “Yesmin Krishno divamvyataha, Tasmat Eeva pratipannam Kaliyugam”. It is widely believed by various people that the universe was created on this day. As per Hindu mythology, Lord Brahma is the creator of the Universe. It is believed that he began creating the world on this day. Further, it is said that Lord Brahma created time, day, and date on this auspicious moment. Hence, Yugadi is celebrated, as it marks the beginning of the universe.

The Lunar calendars have a cycle of sixty years and a New Year starts on the day of Yugadi on Chaitra Shuddha Paadyami. After the completion of every sixty years, the calendar starts with a fresh New Year.

The calendar dates back to the Shalivahana era, which is supposed to have been built by the great legend Shalivahana. King Shalivahana was also referred to as Gautamiputra Satakarni and is responsible for initiating the era of Shalivahana.

The festival of Yugadi marks the beginning of the New Year for those living between the Kaveri River and Vindhyas and those who follow the lunar calendar, particularly in South India in the states of Karnataka, Andhra Pradesh and Telangana. Maharashtra celebrates the same day as Gudi Padwa.

The traditional Yugadi festival is nine days long and centers around a holy ritual known as the Panchanga Shravanam, which is performed by priests in the local temples. This ritual consists of chanting mantras and reading out predictions for the new year.

There are seven traditional rituals associated with Yugadi which the locals perform. Each of these rituals symbolizes the shedding of the old or the welcoming of the new, along with paying tribute to God and seeking his blessing in the new year.

The preparations and decorations for Yugadi point to its symbolic nature of a new beginning. As the beginning of the mango season in the region coincides with Yugadi, the locals decorate the floors of their houses with mango leaves. Fresh flowers also adorn these homes as a symbol of spring, hope and new life.

An amalgamation of Bevu (Neem) and Bella (Jaggery) is traditionally eaten during Yugadi. The six different tastes that make up this combination symbolize the different phases and emotions one goes through in life, as well as the temporary nature of life itself.
