The Rise: A Shot in the Arm for the US Dollar

The decade of 1940s was a tumultuous period in the world. The deadliest conflict in human history, World War II was raging on which had staggering consequences for the human civilization. The conflict claimed the lives of 80 million people, destroyed the pre-war industrial base, and destabilized the financial system of the world. The economic powerhouses of Europe – The United Kingdom and Germany bore the brunt of the economic consequences. The war accelerated the decline of the British Empire which was once described as the “empire on which the sun never sets”. The manufacturing base of UK was bombed to rubble by the German Luftwaffe blitzkrieg. Likewise, the Allies bombed the industrial bases of Germany and other Axis powers. While much of Europe and Asia was devastated, the United States despite being a party to the same war was relatively unscathed. The world financial system had reached an inflection point - the lack of an economic counterbalancing force to the United States during the post war period created a power vacuum, which elevated the dollar’s prominence and eventually led to its rise as the reserve currency of the world.

The Gold Standard and Bretton Woods Agreement

Before the 1950s, floating currencies was the norm where the exchange rate of a currency was determined by supply and demand factors relative to other currencies. But the impact of WW2 had increased the foreign exchange rate volatility. This combined with the monetary chaos during the interwar period exacerbated the financial and political tensions between the leading power of the world. To mitigate this problem, post-war countries sought to establish multilateral economic cooperation which would foster collaboration and growth. This initiative gave birth to the Bretton Woods System and the International Monetary Fund. More on this later, we’ll have to first understand the Gold Standard and its implications.

The Gold Standard is a monetary system where the value of a country's currency is directly linked to a fixed quantity of gold. Under the gold standard, individuals and institutions could exchange paper currency for gold at a fixed rate set by the government or central bank. In a typical gold standard system, the government or central bank would promise to exchange a certain amount of gold for a fixed amount of currency. This exchange rate would determine the value of the currency in relation to gold. If a country's central bank had a fixed exchange rate of $10 for one ounce of gold, for example, then the value of the currency would be determined by the market value of an ounce of gold. Gold Standard had its own advantages and disadvantages, but it addresses two critical issues:

  • Fixed Exchange Rates – Exchange rates would become fixed and stable as the value of each currency was linked to the price of gold.
  • Limited Money Supply – The money supply was limited because the amount of currency in circulation was directly linked to the amount of the underlying asset (gold) held by central banks. This ensured that banks could not print an unlimited amount of money, thereby keeping inflation under control.

The United States held 2/3rd of the gold reserves in the world during the 1940s and hence was in a perfect position to leverage the gold standard for the dollar.

Bretton Woods Conference – On 22nd July 1944, representatives of 44 countries convened in Bretton Woods, New Hampshire, in the United States on to sign the Bretton Woods agreement. The agreement was signed by representatives of the major Allied powers, including the United States, Great Britain, France, and the Soviet Union, as well as smaller nations from around the world. The main reason for the Bretton Woods Agreement was to provide a stable framework for international trade and investment by establishing fixed exchange rates between currencies. The Bretton Woods Agreement also established the International Monetary Fund (IMF), which was designed to help countries maintain their exchange rates and provide a system for international payments. The IMF was tasked with providing loans to countries facing balance of payments difficulties and promoting international economic cooperation. The Bretton Woods System provided several advantages to the US:

  • Reserve Currency Status - The agreement established the US dollar as the world's reserve currency, which gave the US significant influence over global financial and economic affairs. This status allowed the US to print money to finance its own economic growth, while also providing a stable currency for international trade and investment.
  • Economic Growth - By providing a stable international monetary system that supported trade and investment, the Bretton Woods system contributed to the post-World War II economic boom in the United States. This aided economic growth and job creation, fuelling the country's rise as a global economic powerhouse.
  • Reduced Currency Risk - Countries were able to reduce currency risk in international trade and investment by pegging their currencies to the US dollar. This made it easier for businesses to conduct cross-border transactions, which aided in the promotion of international trade and economic integration.
  • Increased Financial Dominance - The Bretton Woods system helped to cement the US's financial dominance, as the country became the centre of global finance and a leading provider of loans and aid to other countries. This dominance enabled the United States to advance its geopolitical interests while also promoting its values and ideals around the world.

While the Bretton Woods Agreement provided significant benefits to the United States, it also imposed significant responsibilities and obligations on the country, such as maintaining a stable currency and providing economic assistance to other countries.

This system eventually proved to be unsustainable for the United States. As a result, the United States unilaterally rescinded the Bretton Woods Agreement. Even though this move undermined confidence in the US Dollar, it continued to rule the roost for the next four decades due to its prevalence and dominance in financial markets - 80% of global trade in the 1970s was in dollars. As a result, the dollar has established hegemony in financial markets.
