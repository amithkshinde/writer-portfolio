The word ‘hung’ is used when the situation has reached an impasse, meaning there is a deadlock in place. In political context, there are countless instances when there is a ‘hung’ legislature with no party getting a clear majority. In such cases, the single largest party will have the first say, wooing the independents or the smaller parties to prove the numbers. After the government is set in place after the floor test, the opposition would be licking their lips to break the ruling party that is hanging by a thread. In such instance, the government is termed to have lost their stake and a no-confidence motion is floated by the leader of opposition. Once this is introduced in the parliament, the ruling party will have to prove its majority to prevent the collapse of the government. Even though this provision is well documented and practiced, the things would not have taken a turn for good, if not for the SR Bommai vs Union of India in the 1990s. This not only protected the federal structure of the country but also strengthened it by reminding the Center government of its constitutional limitations.

Prelude

The Assembly elections of 1985 saw Janata Dal form the government in Karnataka after winning 139 seats of 224 under the able leadership of Ramakrishna Hegde. Everything was going well until the allegation of phone tapping came into the fore and the incumbent took the moral high ground by denouncing his position. This meant that Minister of Industries, S R Bommai was elevated to the CM post on 13th August 1988, but things took a dark turn when the then governor, P Venkatasubbiah dismissed the government. This was done based on major defections from the Janata party after an internal turmoil. Even after several requests from the Chief Minister for a floor test, the governor invoked Article 356 and recommended ‘President Rule’ in the state, which the President of India approved. This perturbed S R Bommai who filed a writ petition in the high court as well as in the Supreme Court, appealing against the abuse of 356. But what followed changed the political landscape for good.

What’s Article 356?

The constitution of India is broadly divided into several parts among which the 18th part speaks of emergency provisions. Classifying it further, the Articles 352, 353, 354, 358 and 359 pertains to emergency due to external forces or internal rebellions whereas Articles 355, 356 and 357 speak about President’s rule in the States during emergency while Article 360 is invoked only during financial emergency.

The Article 356 Clause 1 states that the President of India has the authority to step in when there is failure of constitutional machinery based on a report received from the Governor of the state. If the President finds the report satisfactory, he is allowed to refer to the three sub clauses and act upon it accordingly.

The Article 356 of the Indian constitution as is:

“(1) If the President, on receipt of a report from the Governor of a State or otherwise, is satisfied that a situation has arisen in which the Government of the State cannot be carried on in accordance with the provisions of this Constitution, the President may by Proclamation –
(a) assume to himself all or any of the functions of the Government of the State and all or any of the powers vested in or exercisable by the Governor or any body or authority in the State other than the Legislature of the State;
(b) declare that the powers of the Legislature of the State shall be exercisable by or under the authority of Parliament;
(c) make such incidental and consequential provisions as appear to the President to be necessary or desirable for giving effect to the objects of the Proclamation, including provisions for suspending in whole or in part the operation of any provisions of this Constitution relating to any body or authority in the State:
Provided that nothing in this clause shall authorize the president to assume to himself any of the powers vested in or exercisable by a high court, or to suspend in whole or in part the operation of any provision of this constitution relating to High Courts”.

Historical Background of Article 356

The Article 356 was born out of Section 93 of the Government of India Act, 1935 which provided complete authority to the Governor of a province to discharge the government of its powers including the ministry and the legislature, if an unprecedented situation arises. The only exception was that the Governor was not allowed to stomp on the powers of High Court. This was introduced by the British because they feared that the provincial bodies or the local ministries would take full-monty of the powers given to them, enabling them to overthrow governments from within. Even though it looked like the British were slowly transferring the power, the country was still not out of the imperialistic woods in 1935. Despite it being so controversial, the Article 356 was formed keeping in view that we were a young democratic nation and any untoward event after the independence should not compromise the security of the nation keeping in mind the diversity and the ethnicity of this country.

The constituent assembly did not take this proposal lying down, as few members pointed out that this was totally opposed to the idea of India being federalist democracy. To this Dr B R Ambedkar replied by saying that such articles will never be called into operation and that it would remain a ‘dead letter’. He also added that when such an occasion arises, the President of India would take necessary precautions before suspending the administrative powers of the state. But the Sarkaria Commission setup in 1983 to examine the center-state relationship mentions in its report mentions that the Article 356 was bought into action for more than 75 times in 37 years. What was once considered a ‘dead letter’, was abused by turning it into a political tool. It was important to note that the period of 1975-79 saw the Article invoked 21 times followed by period of 1980-87 where it was abused on 18 occasions.

Abuse of Article 356

As mentioned previously, the ruling party in center had made a merry out of Article 356 in the 70s and the 80s. From 1971 to 1990, it was invoked on 63 occasions with the Janata government bringing down governments in 12 states in 1977 followed by Indira Gandhi led Congress in 1980 when President rule was imposed in nine different states. The years 1991 and 1992 also saw the Center use Babri fiasco as a farce to bring down governments in 9 states including Uttar Pradesh. All in all, the Congress has been responsible for more than 73% of the President’s rule in the country followed by Janata government who imposed it on 9 occasions, after being in power for just two years. The incessant use of this provision was put a full-stop after the landmark verdict in SR Bommai vs Union of India case, where the judges reminded the Central government of its duty to take the states along and to refrain from using the power vested in them.

SR Bommai’s case verdict

After the writ getting rejected in the High Court, S R Bommai appealed against enforcing Article 356 in the state. The Supreme Court took it upon itself and formed a nine-member bench as similar issue came up in the states of Uttar Pradesh, Nagaland, Rajasthan, Himachal Pradesh, and Meghalaya. The arguments for the Bommai’s case started in October 1993 and went on till the end of December 1993. Given the gravity of the situation and the blatant misuse of the power by the Center, the Supreme Court laid six guidelines in front of the legislation of the Center. It also stressed that the power of the president is not absolute and is open to judicial review. This was done to make sure that the Federal fabric is intact and the States are treated courteously before snatching the power.

The six-point guideline laid out by the Supreme Court bench said the following:

  1. The Article 356 should be enforced only in unprecedented times when the governance becomes nearly impossible by making sure that there is no disharmony between the Union and the States.
  2. The State Assembly cannot be dissolved before approval from both Lok Sabha and Rajya Sabha in the parliament.
  3. Even after approval from the legislation, the proclamation is open to judicial review under Article 226 (gives High Courts the ability to issue orders and writs to any person or authority, including the government) and Article 32 (gives the Supreme Court the same power).
  4. The Courts also reserve the authority to review the records from the Government based on which the proclamation was approved.
  5. The Courts can put an interim stay on the exercise of power under Article 356.
  6. The Courts also have the ability to restore the dissolved state assembly and thereby the government, if the proclamation is deemed unconstitutional.

After this landmark judgement, the Center government has been cautious on numerous occasions before invoking the President’s rule. In the recent times, the Article is imposed only when the situation is out of control or when the Assembly is hung because of a split in the ruling party (Uttarakhand 2016, Maharashtra 2019, and Jammu Kashmir 2019). Even though it took more than 45 years for the law to step in and correct it, the Article 356 is relevant when used sparingly. As the saying goes, “With great power, comes great responsibility”, it is a double edged sword.
