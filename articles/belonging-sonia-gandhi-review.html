'Italian Bahu', 'Super PM' etc are some of the names that have been given to Sonia. But who is Sonia Gandhi? Nobody really knows. There are a few theories floating around the internet, and a few books as well. But yet, who she is has always been a closely guarded secret.

Now we have some relief to those questions from 'Belonging: A Journey of Love', a memoir of Sonia, where she herself takes up the questions and answers them. Published by HarperCollins India on 10th November, the book calls the 1975 Emergency a mistake, explains why she said no to the Prime Minister's chair in 2004, and denies that she ran Dr. Manmohan Singh's government from behind.

A book that almost didn't come out

This book first came into the limelight when Penguin Random House India reportedly dropped the memoir after she refused some editorial changes. This is something publishing houses have every right to ask for. And if the author does not comply with the requests, they can drop the book. Penguin, for its part, has denied that this is why the deal ended, and said that fact checks and edits are a normal part of publishing. But the Indian National Congress cried foul over this, and claimed that this was an attempt at silencing dissent, and at making sure that the tallest voice of the opposition i.e. Sonia Gandhi's voice was kept away from the public.

Further, HarperCollins took up the mantle of publishing the book, and it came out on 10th November. Once people got their hands on it, no doubt it was flying off the shelves. But it raised more questions against the Congress and the Nehru-Gandhi family than it answered. It opened some old wounds which had scabbed over, and now those have become bleeding topics that are being questioned in the public forum.

What she says

The big claims are the ones people have been waiting decades to hear from her. On the Emergency, she calls it a mistake, and says that Mrs. Indira Gandhi later felt the same. But the blame, almost all of it, goes to Sanjay Gandhi, who she says Indira should have held back, while Rajiv was against it. She even recalls Sanjay saying at a family dinner, "They must be stopped." Sanjay died in 1980, so he is not here to answer.

On 2004, she says she turned down the Prime Minister's post because Rahul and Priyanka feared for her safety, and because she felt Dr. Manmohan Singh, a man of integrity who did not belong to any camp in the party, would reassure the allies. On the ten years of UPA, she denies being the 'back-seat driver' that critics called her, and says that she and Dr. Singh had an informal division of work, and that she never interfered with files.

Further, she writes that in 2010 Mr. Pranab Mukherjee told her "it was time for a change at the top", which she read as a hint that he wanted the top job. And on Mr. Narendra Modi, she says she has had "practically no real contact" with him in his 12 years as Prime Minister.

Where the problems begin

The book begins with her childhood in Italy, her parents and her family out there. It then goes on to her moving to Cambridge, where she ran into the Gandhi scion, Mr. Rajiv Gandhi. They met, fell in love, and eventually got married in 1968, her wedding sari woven from yarn that Pt. Jawaharlal Nehru had spun in prison. Uptil this point it is all lovey-dovey.

From the next point onwards come the actual problems with the book. Since it's a memoir, she can and should write what she feels, no one is questioning that. But the facts cannot be falsified. Facts that courts have come to on evidence, facts that people have seen, because anything that has happened since she came to India is something people have seen, and have lived. You cannot falsify living memory.

Take 1984. She writes about Operation Blue Star, about finding grenades in a room of the house, about Indira crying over photographs of the damaged Golden Temple, and about Rahul and Priyanka being brought back from boarding school because of kidnapping fears. Then Indira was shot dead on 31st October 1984, and the anti-Sikh violence followed. The book talks about the delay in calling the Army, and answers the charges about Rajiv's role. But for the families who lived through those days, and who have waited 40 years for justice, her version is just one side of the story.

Even the simple facts are slipping. The Tribune has pointed out that the book gives 1921 as the year the RSS was founded, when it was 1925. It puts Mr. Ahmed Patel's death in 2022, when he died in November 2020. And it dates the Anna Hazare movement to 2012, when it was 2011. These are small, no doubt. But this is the same book where the author reportedly refused the publisher's changes, so the errors only add to the questions.

What it leaves out

What she does not say is as loud as what she says. Mr. Anand Ranganathan has pointed out that there is no chapter on Bofors or Ottavio Quattrocchi. A Moneycontrol panel with Mr. Nalin Mehta has flagged the silence on Kashmir and 26/11. Ms. Shobhaa De says it goes light on 1984 and the Bhopal gas tragedy, and calls the book "more Mills & Boon than Machiavelli". And on India Today, Mr. Rajdeep Sardesai's editors' roundtable asked whether she "played safe" on Shah Bano, Babri Masjid and Gujarat 2002.

What others are saying

Not everyone is against it. Ms. Sobhana K Nair of The Hindu called it "very well written", with "sparkling prose", on Newslaundry's Hafta. Mr. Vir Sanghvi, on ThePrint, said it is not really a political book at all, but three books in one.

But the critics are louder. Mr. Abhinandan Sekhri said that political memoirs are rarely honest while the author is still in politics. Mr. Anand Vardhan doubted that she wrote it herself. Mr. Abhijit Iyer-Mitra, on the Times of India's AXIS, called it a "political blunder", and accused it of "throwing muck at the dead", meaning Sanjay Gandhi and Pranab Mukherjee, who cannot reply. And Ms. De made the sharpest point of all, that by turning down the top job, Sonia got to hold power without ever having to answer for it.

The rest of the story

Further, the book covers her tears while watching the Babri Masjid come down in 1992, which she calls a turning point for the BJP and the RSS. It covers her years of holding back from politics after Rajiv was killed in 1991, becoming Congress President in 1998, and the 1999 revolt by senior leaders over her foreign origin. It covers the falling out with Maneka Gandhi after Sanjay's death, and Maneka leaving the house in 1982.

There are personal chapters too. The fear she had of Indira's temper in the early years. Losing her first child, born premature, in 1969. And her fight with breast cancer in 2010, and a growth on her pancreas a year later, for which she had surgery near Milan, at a time when the public only knew that she was abroad.
