Now that we have a pretty decent bird’s eye view of the Moon’s orbital mechanics, we can further probe into the method of finding moon’s approximate location in your sky without even having to look up. We’re going to need some basic math. That’s all it takes!

For our fairly simple calculations here, we’ll have to assume that the moon’s orbit around Earth is a perfect circle (when in reality, it is not. Moon’s orbit is an ellipse or an elongated circle). Also, we’re going to presume that it takes exactly 30 days for the moon to go around us once (There are multiple definitions of a month with the orbital period varying accordingly with the definition).

We all know that a circle has 360°, and it takes 30 days for the moon to trudge through entirely. This implies that the moon will move about 12° each day (360°/30 = 12°). As seen from earth’s North Pole, the moon orbits us counter-clockwise (which means any observer on earth will find it moving eastward; the same direction as the rotation and revolution of earth).

Earth completes one full rotation around its axis (360°) in 24 hours. This means we rotate 15° every hour (360°/24 = 15°; a little less than an hour for 12° rotation, about 50 minutes).

So when on a certain day, the moon being situated in an arbitrary spot in the sky, by the next day, it would’ve moved 12° eastward in its orbit. So the earth has to rotate through that extra 12° in order to bring the moon back to the same spot in the sky as it was on the previous day. This takes an additional 50 minutes of time. As a consequence of this, the moon rises (and therefore sets) about 50 minutes later each day. If you know where the moon was on the preceding night, it will just be 12° east (towards your eastern horizon) of that place in the sky (for reference, the human fist held at arm’s length takes up about 10° of the sky), on the following night. If you know the time of moonrise on a particular day, you can predict the same for the next day (add 50 minutes). Using these parameters, it’s safe to say that you can fairly triangulate the moon’s approximate location in the sky and surprise your friends with this simple trick!

Have you ever looked up at the moon and wondered why you can only see one face/side of it, day after day? Well, it’s not just another coincidence. There’s a reason for it. This is because the moon rotates, but it takes precisely as long for the moon to spin on its axis as it does to complete its monthly orbit around earth (the moon rotates once every 30 days). As a result, the moon never turns its back to us, like a dancer circle, but always facing its partner. This phenomenon, called Synchronous Tidal Locking, sounds like a weird coincidence, but it’s actually quite common. All the solar system’s large moons are tidally locked with their planets.

Tidal locking is the most stable state of rotation for any two large spherical bodies in consideration. In the infant stages of our solar system when the earth’s moon was formed after a massive object collided with earth, gravity brought in all the broken pieces together. This object we now call our moon, would have been spinning wildly, with uneven distribution of matter all around. The side closest to earth would have encountered a huge gravitational pull from the earth in comparison to the side farthest from earth experiencing a lesser gravitational pull. This would shift the center of gravity slightly toward the earth, away from the moon’s center of mass (the average of all the moon’s mass concentrated at one point). The moon would still force its rotation overcoming this drag from earth.

The part of the moon that was pulled toward earth would have shifted as the moon spun, but always at a delay, since it takes time for so much material to rise and then later fall. This means the moon’s bulge was always a little out of alignment with earth, yet always being pulled toward alignment by gravity. As the moon bent and fluxed in this tug of war, energy was released in the form of heat (the same tidal heating mechanism is responsible for the geologic activity of the most volcanically active body in the solar system: Io, one of Jupiter’s Galilean moons). As the energy dissipated, the moon’s rotation slowed until a single spin on its axis took the same amount of time as one trip around earth. In this state, the bulge on the moon was no longer shifting relative to the earth, therefore no more energy needed to be dissipated by this particular process, and the spin rate stopped changing.

At the same time as energy was being burned off within the moon and slowing its rotation, energy was being added to the moon’s orbit by similar tidal forces, causing the moon to slowly drift away from Earth.

The same process is happening even now. The moon continues to move away from earth at a rate of about an inch-and-a-half (4 cm) per year, its drift slowing as it goes. The energy propelling it away comes primarily from earth’s oceans, which both bulge out in response to the moon’s gravity and exert a gravitational pull of their own on the moon, resulting in ocean tides here on earth. Earth’s bulging oceans don’t exactly match up with the position of the moon, they’re always a little out of sync because it takes time for all that water to shift and pile up. This interaction creates friction that slows earth’s own rotation, and creates forces that change the moon’s orbital speed, causing it to fall farther away into space.

About 50 billion years from now, if the moon and earth could somehow survive the eventual death of the sun, the moon would be so far away, and its orbit so large, that earth would also tidally lock to the moon. Only the population of one lucky half of our world would ever see the Moon.

As the moon orbits around earth, we should also understand this from the moon’s point-of-view. If you stood on the moon (on the side facing earth), and looked at earth each night (or day; it won’t matter because the moon doesn’t have an atmosphere to scatter any sunlight, so you can still see the earth during daytime), the earth would also cycle through all the phases that we tag to the moon from down here, but in the opposite order of events. You take a trip to the moon (during a new moon phase), arrive at Tranquility Base (where the first humans walked on the moon’s surface during Apollo 11), and look for the elusive Blue Marble in your sky. You’ll be served with a visual treat as you gaze upon a full earth’s disc shining back at you, with all the oceans, continents and clouds, reflecting all the sunlight. The earth appears about four times larger in the lunar sky than the moon appears in earth's sky. Overall, the moon reflects only 11% of the sunlight striking it, but the earth reflects approximately 37% of the sunlight incident upon it. Put this all together, and a "full Earth" as seen from the moon is about 43 times brighter than the full moon is, as seen from earth. Yes, when there’s a new moon, there’s going to be a Full Earth (as seen from the moon). Earth and moon essentially mirror each other’s phases.

When the moon is just a sophomore, just a few days after its new moon phase, and you start seeing a thin crescent appearing, the earth would be sparkling in the moon’s sky. This is the best time to look for Earthshine. When you see the crescent moon with just a thin sliver of its surface being lit by the sun, you will also notice the remaining disc of the moon glimmering faintly (but bright enough to stand out). Strictly speaking, this side of the moon (not lit by sunlight) shouldn’t be visible as it is not reflecting any sunlight per se. What you’re seeing is, some of the light being reflected off of a full earth, scampers through to strike the moon’s surface, gets ricocheted off the moon and reach your eyes. This earth-reflected sunlight bouncing off from the crescent moon will render the dark portion of the moon (the part not lit by sunlight) visible, glowing faintly with steady brightness outlining the moon’s full disc. This is why this phenomenon is rightly termed ‘Earthshine’. The faint side of the crescent moon is essentially the earth [light] shining on the moon. A full earth (as seen from the moon) is acting like a secondary source of light on top of the sun. Earthshine is most commonly observed during the crescent phases (both waxing; after sunset in the west and waning; before sunrise in the east). This is because, only during these times in its orbit, not much of the moon’s surface will be beaming sunlight back to us here on earth. When the moon gets to a first quarter/third quarter and their respective gibbous phases, at least half (more than half during the gibbous phases) of the moon’s disc will be reflecting sunlight and any earth-reflected sunlight making it through to the moon and coming back towards us will not survive the bright glow of sunlight on the moon. Sunlight is simply much brighter than the light reflected off of earth and striking the lunar surface.

So the next time you’re out for a late evening stroll, dining upon the crimson sunset and nautical twilight view, and happen to meet your eyes with the crescent moon, just shimmy to the other side of the moon, you’ll find a faint glow highlighting the silhouette of a full moon. Try to think of all the factors falling into place to portray this cosmic spectacle to you.

When you’re watching Earthshine unfold, you are practically seeing yourself embedded into the moon. The light photons from the sun that are bumping into you, get reflected off of you all the way to the moon and back to your eyes. The Cosmos is within us. We are made of star-stuff.
